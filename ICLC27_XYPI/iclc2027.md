# Background

## Data-driven sonification with maps

Sonification refers to the definition of systematic relationships between data and sound parameters, i.e., using audio and music to represent data. While originally theorized by Kramer, establishing an early interdisciplinary foundation for Auditory Display (AD) [@kramer1994auditory], it later developed into a broader discipline involving several related methods and techniques [@hermann2008taxonomy; @hermann2011handbook]. Overall, parameter-mapping sonification arguably constitutes one of the most common approaches: Dubus and Bresin reviewed previous work and identified peculiar recurring mappings, such as data-to-pitch [@dubus2013systematic], while Ben-Tal and Berger discussed the relation between "informational" sonification - i.e., make data more explicit using sound without imposing any particular musical direction - and proper musical composition exploiting data in the process [@bental2004creative].

With specific regards to maps and cartography sonification, audio has been widely applied to geographic information for navigation, accessibility, or spatial exploration. Early auditory and tactile maps investigated non-visual access to geographic spaces [@blasch1973auditory; @jacobson1998navigating; @delogu2010nonvisual]; systems such as *AudioGPS* [@holland2002audiogps] and *gpsTunes* [@strachan2005gpstunes] connected geographic movement to spatial audio with adaptive musical playback; or Krygier, in cartographic visualization, proposed sound as an extension of the representational vocabulary of maps [@krygier1994sound]. Many subsequent works explored the sonification of different kinds of geographic data, including remote-sensing reliability [@fisher1994hearing], geo-referenced data [@zhao2004sonification], choropleth maps [@zhao2005interactive], GIS information [@bearman2012using], or other web-based geographic datasets [@foteinou2022sonification; @biggs2022evaluation].
Other works considered geographic/urban information as material for musical interaction, relevant examples including *Sonic City*, which connected movement and environmental sensing to real-time sound generation in an urban context [@gaye2003sonic]; *COMPath*, which adopted an online map to retrieve geographically situated information and convert route into musical events [@park2010composition]. Other works explored similar approaches within ubiquitous-music/smart-city frameworks: for instance, Sarmento *et al.* used urban and environmental data as input to musical processes [@sarmento2022ubiquitous]; Dama focused on location-based composition and geographic orientation [@dama2021audio]; Levy-Landesberg investigated sound maps and the relationship between sound and place [@levylandesberg2022sound]; or Turbitt *et al.* used geo-tagged sound material and a browser-based map for dynamic soundscape composition [@turbitt2024promenadologie]. 

In this field, OpenStreetMap [@haklay2008openstreetmap] provides an especially relevant infrastructure, combining street geometry, network topology, and semantically-tagged geographic entities within a collaboratively-maintained dataset. This indeed supports the use of routes and urban points of interest as structured geographic data.


## Live coding behaviours beyond patterns

A large part of live coding practice is generally organized around the manipulation of temporal patterns [@collins2003live]. Notorious examples includes widespread environments such as TidalCycles, which represents musical patterns as functions relating time and events [@mclean2014tidal]; Strudel, which later transferred this model to a browser-based environment combining pattern programming, execution, and visualization [@roos2023strudel]; or Magnusson's ixi lang, providing a compact vocabulary over SuperCollider for rapid musical manipulation [@magnusson2011ixi]. In these approaches, live coders mainly build up the performance by defining and transforming musical structures over time.

More recently, however, live coding practices have also expanded beyond the mere declaration of musical structures, starting to incorporate processes, rules, and behaviours as performative material. A prominent direction concerns choreography and embodied performance. Sicchio used programming as a real-time choreographic score, with code defining structures subsequently interpreted through bodily movement [@sicchio2014hacking], and subsequently formalized *Terpsicode*, a proper language for real-time choreographic programming [@sicchio2022terpsicode]. Similar works have extended these ideas to mixed human-machine performance, including live notation for movement [@mclean2024notation], or platforms for live human-robot choreography based on high-level spatial and gestural abstractions [@martin2024prototype; @sicchio2025robot].
A second strand concerns autonomous and agent-based systems, where musical structure emerges from computational processes and interactions rather than being specified directly. For example, Bovermann and Griffiths treated computation and self-modifying processes as performance material [@bovermann2014computation]; Blackwell and Young used particle swarms and stigmergic interaction to model musical self-organization [@blackwell2004selforganized]; or Eigenfeldt and Pasquier developed ecosystems of autonomous musical agents whose local interactions generate larger-scale musical structures [@eigenfeldt2011ecosystem]. Within live coding specifically, Stewart and Lawson's Cibo autonomously performed TidalCycles code [@stewart2019cibo], and their newer Cibo v2 extended this approach through a real-time agent, trained on multiple live coders' sets [@stewart2020cibov2]. Other systems have explored virtual companions proposing musical material [@xambo2021cloud] or advanced agents capable to suggest and execute "disruptive" transformations during performance [@attanayake2020disruption].
Spatial behaviour provides another viewpoint on these approaches. Roma, for example, explores live-coded musical systems in which agents move through two-dimensional environments, coupling trajectories and spatial relations with sound synthesis [@roma2023agent], and later extends this approach to flocking systems whose collective sonic behaviour emerges from live-coded interaction rules [@roma2025flocks]. 

Most importantly, these systems are peculiar because they also raise broader questions about the role of computational agency in performance: virtual agents blurry the distribution of authorship and control between performer and system [@xambo2022virtual], while machine listening introduces additional forms of responsiveness and liveness [@diapoulis2023liveness]. Consequently, more recent work started to frame live coding in terms of negotiated human-machine agency beyond the traditional perspective of the computational systems as mere instruments executing instructions [@xambo2024agencies].



# Design Motivation and Metaphor
The design of the system emerged from a broader question: **how can a map function not simply as a representation, but as a structure for musical thought and interaction?** Maps necessarily abstract and organize relations between entities, locations, and trajectories, making them quite suitable for reasoning about spatial behaviours. From a more philosophical perspective, this idea resonates with processual accounts of cartography, in which maps are understood as objects continually produced through their use [@kitchin2007rethinking]; also, in their book *A Thousand Plateaus: Capitalism and Schizophrenia*, Deleuze and Guattari similarly characterize the map as open to connection, modification, and experimentation [@deleuze1987thousand]. We can speculate that such views may be indeed compatible with live coding, where structures remain operational while being continuously inspected and transformed.
The metaphor also reflects our broader experience with live coding and with the use of maps in compositional contexts. More specifically, the reasoning behind the present system emerged from considering *GeoMuse Beta*\footnote{\url{https://www.geomusebeta.com/}} and *Promenadologie* [@turbitt2024promenadologie]. Although very different in scope (the former being a generative live environment, the latter a soundwalk listening platform), both suggest that spatial structures can become more than interfaces for visualizing musical information: paths, positions, and geographical relations can actively participate in organizing musical experience.
This also raised a more fundamental design question: **which properties actually make a map meaningful as an interaction space?** In our view, extracting generic visual features from a map would not be of sufficient metaphoric strength: for example, if a system operated only on visual-defined contours or edges, little would distinguish a map from any other image containing comparable shapes. We therefore focused instead on explicitly cartographic relations: proximity, adjacency, connectivity, trajectories, regions, movement, and relative position. These properties are meaningful because they describe how entities inhabit and move through a structured space, and they suggest forms of exploration that are inherently map-like rather than merely visual.

Building on these ideas, we treat the map as an executable musical environment rather than solely as a representation of physical territory. Locations, trajectories, distances, and movements become abstractions through which computational entities and their behaviours can be described, observed, and manipulated. The map therefore serves simultaneously as a visual metaphor, an organizational structure, and a performative space for live coding.

An `area` selects a connected portion of the street graph. The agent can move through streets belonging to that territory. A `line` is interpreted as a street-following corridor routed through the control coordinates. A `points` declaration  defines an attraction field over the street graph rather than a route connecting the points.

## Mapping


An event is generated when an agent reaches a meaningful street node. The current prototype exports three principal continuous values:

- normalized vertical position → pitch-related control;
- normalized horizontal position → timbre-related control;
- measured traversal time of the previous street segment → duration-related control.

The duration is therefore not selected from a metrical pattern. If an edge has metric length $d$ and the agent travels at speed $v$, its nominal traversal time is $d/v$, while the actual event reports the measured traversal time.
