# Introduction

Maps and other geographic data have a long tradition of relation with sound. Research in Auditory Display and sonification has explored the translation of geographic data into auditory representations [@krygier1994sound; @zhao2005interactive; @bearman2012using], while several musical systems have treated and urban information as material for composition and interaction [@gaye2003sonic; @park2010composition; @sarmento2022ubiquitous]. These approaches in the musical field suggest a broader question that motivates the present work: can a map become not only something to sonify, but a structure through which musical processes themselves can be organized?

In the context of live coding, the large part of the practice involves real-time construction and transformation of musical patterns [@collins2003live; @mclean2014tidal; @roos2023strudel]. At the same time, other approaches have progressively expanded what can be live coded, incorporating e.g. computational behaviours, movement, or autonomous entities as performative material [@bovermann2014computation; @sicchio2014hacking; @roma2023entity]. From this perspective, geographic space offers an interesting substrate: its fixed entities can provide material for pattern construction, while its topology can constrain behaviours whose resulting musical organization is not completely specified in advance.

In this paper, we introduce *XYPI*, a browser-based live coding environment built around these two complementary interpretations of geographic structure. The first, `grid`, retrieves categories of urban points of interest and projects their spatial distribution onto a discrete sequencer, quantizing geography into musical time. The second, `entity`, places moving entities on the actual street network: their trajectories are constrained by street connectivity, while musical events occur as relevant nodes are reached. The two models coexist within the same map, combining metrically synchronized patterns with asynchronous behavioural processes, all determined by geographical data. Positions are mapped to synthesis parameters and transmitted via OSC to SuperCollider, while a simple browser interface integrates the geographic visualization and editable live code within the same performance surface.

Therefore, our contribution is not an entirely new technique for sonifying coordinates, but a prototype exploring maps as live-codable structures. In particular, XYPI brings fixed urban entities and street topology into the same performative framework, exposing different relationships between geography, time, predictability, and control. As identical declarations can also produce different structures when evaluated over different cities or map scales, the geographic context itself becomes part of the executable musical configuration. Through the implementation and preliminary exploration of the system, we examine how these properties may extend familiar pattern-oriented approaches toward forms of live coding in which performers manipulate not only musical structures, but also some of the spatial and behavioural conditions from which those structures emerge.

The remainder of the paper reviews related work in geographic sonification and behavioural live coding, discusses the design motivation behind XYPI, describes its implementation and interaction models, and concludes with preliminary observations on its emerging musical properties and their implications for live coding practice.



# Background

## Data-driven sonification with maps

Sonification refers to the definition of systematic relationships between data and sound parameters, i.e., using audio and music to represent data. While originally theorized by Kramer, establishing an early interdisciplinary foundation for Auditory Display [@kramer1994auditory], it later developed into a broader discipline involving several related methods and techniques [@hermann2008taxonomy; @hermann2011handbook]. Overall, parameter-mapping sonification arguably constitutes one of the most common approaches: Dubus and Bresin reviewed previous work and identified peculiar recurring mappings, such as data-to-pitch [@dubus2013systematic], while Ben-Tal and Berger discussed the relation between "informational" sonification - i.e., make data more explicit using sound without imposing any particular musical direction - and proper musical composition exploiting data in the process [@bental2004creative].

With specific regards to maps and cartography sonification, audio has been widely applied to geographic information for navigation, accessibility, or spatial exploration. Early auditory and tactile maps investigated non-visual access to geographic spaces [@blasch1973auditory; @jacobson1998navigating; @delogu2010nonvisual]; systems such as *AudioGPS* [@holland2002audiogps] and *gpsTunes* [@strachan2005gpstunes] connected geographic movement to spatial audio with adaptive musical playback; or Krygier, in cartographic visualization, proposed sound as an extension of the representational vocabulary of maps [@krygier1994sound]. Many subsequent works explored the sonification of different kinds of geographic data, including remote-sensing reliability [@fisher1994hearing], geo-referenced data [@zhao2004sonification], choropleth maps [@zhao2005interactive], GIS information [@bearman2012using], or other web-based geographic datasets [@foteinou2022sonification; @biggs2022evaluation].
Other works considered geographic/urban information as material for musical interaction, relevant examples including *Sonic City*, which connected movement and environmental sensing to real-time sound generation in an urban context [@gaye2003sonic]; *COMPath*, which adopted an online map to retrieve geographically situated information and convert route into musical events [@park2010composition]. Other works explored similar approaches within ubiquitous-music/smart-city frameworks: for instance, Sarmento *et al.* used urban and environmental data as input to musical processes [@sarmento2022ubiquitous]; Dama focused on location-based composition and geographic orientation [@dama2021audio]; Levy-Landesberg investigated sound maps and the relationship between sound and place [@levylandesberg2022sound]; or Turbitt *et al.* used geo-tagged sound material and a browser-based map for dynamic soundscape composition [@turbitt2024promenadologie]. 

In this field, OpenStreetMap [@haklay2008openstreetmap] provides an especially relevant infrastructure, combining street geometry, network topology, and semantically-tagged geographic entities within a collaboratively-maintained dataset. This indeed supports the use of routes and urban points of interest as structured geographic data.


## Live coding behaviours beyond patterns

A large part of live coding practice is generally organized around the manipulation of temporal patterns [@collins2003live]. Notorious examples includes widespread environments such as TidalCycles, which represents musical patterns as functions relating time and events [@mclean2014tidal]; Strudel, which later transferred this model to a browser-based environment combining pattern programming, execution, and visualization [@roos2023strudel]; or Magnusson's ixi lang, providing a compact vocabulary over SuperCollider for rapid musical manipulation [@magnusson2011ixi]. In these approaches, live coders mainly build up the performance by defining and transforming musical structures over time.

More recently, however, live coding practices have also expanded beyond the mere declaration of musical structures, starting to incorporate processes, rules, and behaviours as performative material. A prominent direction concerns choreography and embodied performance. Sicchio used programming as a real-time choreographic score, with code defining structures subsequently interpreted through bodily movement [@sicchio2014hacking], and subsequently formalized *Terpsicode*, a proper language for real-time choreographic programming [@sicchio2022terpsicode]. Similar works have extended these ideas to mixed human-machine performance, including live notation for movement [@mclean2024notation], platforms for live human-robot choreography based on high-level spatial and gestural abstractions [@martin2024prototype; @sicchio2025robot], and live-coded locomotion and choreography for web-based environments.
A second strand concerns autonomous and entity-based systems, where musical structure emerges from computational processes and interactions rather than being specified directly. For example, Bovermann and Griffiths treated computation and self-modifying processes as performance material [@bovermann2014computation]; Blackwell and Young used particle swarms and stigmergic interaction to model musical self-organization [@blackwell2004selforganized]; or Eigenfeldt and Pasquier developed ecosystems of autonomous musical entities whose local interactions generate larger-scale musical structures [@eigenfeldt2011ecosystem]. Within live coding specifically, Stewart and Lawson's Cibo autonomously performed TidalCycles code [@stewart2019cibo], and their newer Cibo v2 extended this approach through a real-time entity, trained on multiple live coders' sets [@stewart2020cibov2]. Other systems have explored virtual companions proposing musical material [@xambo2021cloud] or advanced entities capable to suggest and execute "disruptive" transformations during performance [@attanayake2020disruption].
Spatial behaviour provides another viewpoint on these approaches. Roma, for example, explores live-coded musical systems in which entities move through two-dimensional environments, coupling trajectories and spatial relations with sound synthesis [@roma2023entity], and later extends this approach to flocking systems whose collective sonic behaviour emerges from live-coded interaction rules [@roma2025flocks]. 

Most importantly, these systems are peculiar because they also raise broader questions about the role of computational agency in performance: virtual entitys blurry the distribution of authorship and control between performer and system [@xambo2022virtual], while machine listening introduces additional forms of responsiveness and liveness [@diapoulis2023liveness]. Consequently, more recent work started to frame live coding in terms of negotiated human-machine agency beyond the traditional perspective of the computational systems as mere instruments executing instructions [@xambo2024agencies].



# Design Motivation and Metaphor
\label{sec:motivation}
The design of the system emerged from a broader question: **how can a map function not simply as a representation, but as a structure for musical thought and interaction?** Maps necessarily abstract and organize relations between entities, locations, and trajectories, making them quite suitable for reasoning about spatial behaviours. From a more philosophical perspective, this idea resonates with processual accounts of cartography, in which maps are understood as objects continually produced through their use [@kitchin2007rethinking]; also, in their book *A Thousand Plateaus: Capitalism and Schizophrenia*, Deleuze and Guattari similarly characterize the map as open to connection, modification, and experimentation [@deleuze1987thousand]. We can speculate that such views may be indeed compatible with live coding, where structures remain operational while being continuously inspected and transformed.
The metaphor also reflects our broader experience with live coding and with the use of maps in compositional contexts. More specifically, the reasoning behind the present system emerged from considering *GeoMuse Beta*\footnote{\url{https://www.geomusebeta.com/}} and *Promenadologie* [@turbitt2024promenadologie]. Although very different in scope (the former being a generative live environment, the latter a soundwalk listening platform), both suggest that spatial structures can become more than interfaces for visualizing musical information: paths, positions, and geographical relations can actively participate in organizing musical experience.
This also raised a more fundamental design question: **which properties actually make a map meaningful as an interaction space?** In our view, extracting generic visual features from a map would not be of sufficient metaphoric strength: for example, if a system operated only on visual-defined contours or edges, little would distinguish a map from any other image containing comparable shapes. We therefore focused instead on explicitly cartographic relations: proximity, adjacency, connectivity, trajectories, regions, movement, and relative position. These properties are meaningful because they describe how entities inhabit and move through a structured space, and they suggest forms of exploration that are inherently map-like rather than merely visual.

Building on these ideas, we thus imagined the map as defining scaffold for a live-coding musical environment, in which trajectories, distances, and places become abstractions through which different musical processes can be described and organized, and constitute their complex of affordances and constraints. Indeed, after several discussions and early prototyping, we eventually focused on two complementary forms of cartographic information: **streets** and **places**. Streets implicitly define networks of possible movement, connecting locations while constraining the trajectories that can be followed; places, on the contrary, represent fixed urban infrastructures (e.g., hospitals, restaurants, libraries...) characterized by their identity, location, and distribution across space. This distinction provides the basis for the two musical models developed in the system: street connectivity supports autonomous entities whose behaviour unfolds through movement across the network, while fixed points of interest are transformed into spatially derived, metrically sequenced patterns. The map thus simultaneously takes on the role of a visual metaphor, an organizational structure, and a performative space, where different properties of geographic space can produce different forms of musical organization.

## Storytelling through geospatial sonification

Beyond its role as a live-coding instrument, XYPI has emerged as a tool for data storytelling and research communication. The framework's ability to translate geographic patterns directly into audible structures creates possibilities for sonifying complex spatial datasets and research findings. In experimental work conducted during the kickoff of the CITY-AI project [@isti2024cityai], we have explored using geospatial sonification through XYPI and its predecessors (GeoMuse) to communicate research results from interdisciplinary studies on urban dynamics. For instance, we sonified data from research on dynamic gentrification models [@mauro2025gentrification], where geographic clustering of demographic and economic indicators could be rendered as musical density and pitch relationships. Similarly, we have sonified research on mobility constraints in segregation models [@gambetta2023mobility], allowing the entity movement patterns in XYPI to directly reflect empirical findings about how spatial mobility preferences influence urban segregation dynamics. These applications demonstrate that geospatial sonification can serve as a bridge between scientific visualization and artistic expression, potentially opening new avenues for public engagement with research through performance and installation.



# System Concept and Implementation

## General Overview

The framework is implemented as a live codable representation of geographic space. From a high-level perspective, gographical data can be used to define two complementary musical layers: **street entitys**, which move through the actual topology of the road network, and **place grids**, which transform fixed points of interest into metrically-quantized sequences. 

Overall, the architecture can be summarized as a relatively simple pipeline. First, geographic data are retrieved from OpenStreetMap and converted into a street graph and collections of place coordinates. These data are visualized within a browser-based interface, where the map is displayed in the background and editable code is overlaid on top of it, according to the typical code-over-visual layout commonly found in A/V live coding frameworks. Interaction is based on a small set of Python-based instructions, designed to describe musical processes in terms of geographic entities and behaviours. Individual musical layers are declared by assigning an `entity(...)` or `grid(...)` instruction to a named connection (e.g., `l1`, `l2`, ...), allowing multiple entitys and grids to coexist and be modified independently within the same map. An `entity` declaration creates a moving entity constrained by the street network, while a `grid` declaration informs a step sequencer with a selected category of places - more about that in the following subsections. Obviously, these instructions can be live coded directly in the browser while the system is running.
A central Python runtime interprets the declarations, maintains the current state of entitys and grids, and sends the resulting musical events via Open Sound Control (OSC)\footnote{\url{https://opensoundcontrol.stanford.edu/}} protocol to SuperCollider\footnote{\url{https://supercollider.github.io/}}, loading a series of simple `SynthDefs` for sound generation.

## Geographic data and graphs

The geographic layer is based on OpenStreetMap [@haklay2008openstreetmap] and it is queried through the Overpass API. The selected geographic window determines both the street network and the points of interest available to the performance.
XYPI is implemented in Python, leveraging the rich ecosystem of geospatial data science libraries, in particular GeoPandas [@geopandas2026]. This choice enables rapid prototyping and integration of complex geographic analyses within a live-coding environment, where performers need immediate feedback on spatial transformations.

In the case of the `entity` layers, OSM streets are converted into a graph, whose nodes correspond to intersections and endpoints, and edges represent the street segments connecting them. Importantly, an edge retains the metric length of the original street. Entities can thus use these information to move along the actual street shape rather than interpolating between graph coordinates (see Section \ref{ssec:entitys}).
The `grid` layers instead use the same geographic window, but retrieves tagged objects rather than street connectivity. The current implementation includes schools, hospitals, restaurants, bars, bus stops, and monuments - but more could be added. Their positions remain fixed: unlike entities, these points do not define movements, but determine the spatial distribution of cells in a grid sequencer (see Section \ref{ssec:grids}).
Finally, all geographic positions are normalized $[0,1]$ to a two-dimensional coordinate system, with horizontal position increasing west-east and vertical position south-north: this representation provides a common interface bridging geographic data, live coded spatial coordinates, browser visualization, and synthesis parameters.



## Entities: moving across streets
\label{ssec:entitys}

The `entity` layer treats the street network as an environment within which autonomous musical entities can move. Once declared, it retrieves street graphs to define the shortest path, and insert moving "entity" on this path, which starts to move according to its assigned parameters. Its spatial vocabulary is loosely inspired by Kandinsky's famous distinction between point, line, and plane [@kandinsky1979point], translated here into the three declarations `points`, `line`, and `area`. These spatial types are interpreted differently according to the underlying graph. For example:

```python
l1 = entity("area", [(0.15,0.15), (0.75,0.15), (0.82,0.72)],
           speed=5, behaviour=random_walk, sound="bass")

l2 = entity("line", [(0.08,0.75), (0.45,0.45)],
           speed=7, behaviour=straightish, sound="hh")

l3 = entity("points", [(0.22,0.25)],
           speed=6, behaviour=point_attract, sound="harmonic")
```

An `area` describes a polygonal region of the map. Streets located inside this region are selected and the entity is allowed to move through the connected street network contained within it - the polygon thus acts as a territorial constraint; a `line` defines a street-following route between a sequence of positions - the entity is constrained to move along the corresponding connected path in the street network; and `points` define one or more attraction targets - the entity is free to move through the graph but its trajectory is biased toward the specified positions. Examples of the three `entity` types are shown in Figure \ref{fig:entitys}.

Once the spatial constraint has been established, movement is controlled by two main parameters: behaviour and speed. The first determines which outgoing street is chosen whenever the entity reaches a relevant street node or intersection, allowing for random motion, directional preferences, backtracking, attraction toward points, or preferences for shorter or longer streets; the second, expressed in (real) metres per second, determines how fast the entity traverses the current street segment. Reaching such a node triggers a musical event, whose duration reflects the traversal time of the preceding street segment. Consequently, the structure of the road network directly affects musical timing: longer streets generally produce longer intervals between events, while denser areas containing shorter segments can generate events more frequently, resulting in a strongly asynchronous temporality.

## Grids: sequencing POI
\label{ssec:grids}

The second mode interprets fixed geographic objects as the content of a step sequencer's cells. It is possible to define a `grid` layer and specify a category of POI from the available ones (i.e., restaurants, hospitals, schools, bars, bus stops, or monuments); at this point, the system retrieves all corresponding coordinates within the current map and projects their normalized positions onto a discrete square grid. For example:

```python
l1 = grid("schools", steps=16, bpm=120,
          direction="horizontal", movement="linear", sound="hh")

l2 = grid("hospitals", steps=13, bpm=92.4,
          direction="vertical", movement="backforth", sound="harmonic")
```

The `steps` parameter controls the sequencer resolution in both dimensions: thus, for instance, `steps=8` produces an $8\times8$ spatial grid, while `steps=16`, a $16\times16$ grid - Figure \ref{fig:grids}. Each POI activates the cell containing its geographic position; if several ones fall into the same cell, it remains a single active sequencer location. Therefore, the current implementation is primarily concerned with the spatial distribution of occupied regions and it's not implemented to represent every individual object as a separate musical event.

As in a traditional sequencer, the grid can be oriented either horizontally or vertically, determining which spatial axis is interpreted as musical time and which organizes the active cells within each step. Multiple grids, including different orientations and POI categories, can coexist on the same map and run independently. Their temporal traversal can then be controlled through familiar step-sequencer behaviours, such as linear progression, back-and-forth movement, or random step selection. Contrary to the `entity` layer, `grid` is metrically quantized and anchored to a specified BPM clock, meaning that the two layers expose deliberately different relationships between geography and musical time. `grid` transforms geographic position into a repeatable pattern, whereas `entity` derives event timing from movement through geographic topology.



## Mapping and Sound

Both layers communicate with SuperCollider through a uniformed OSC event structure:

```text
/xypi/corner  connection_Name  sound_Name  y  x  duration
```

Here, `/xypi/corner` identifies the common musical event message used by the system; `connection_Name` the live-coded layer that produced the event, i.e. names such as `l1`, `l2`, and so on; `sound_Name` specifies the SuperCollider `SynthDef` to be allocated on the server; `x` and `y` contain the geographic coordinates of the event; and `duration` defines its temporal extent.
For entitys, duration corresponds to the traversal time of the previous street segment, computed as $T \approx d/v$ for a segment of length $d$ travelled at speed $v$. For grids, the same spatial coordinates are transmitted, but duration is determined by the clock, with $T_{\mathrm{step}} = (60/B)k$ for tempo $B$ and $k$ beats per step.

For the current implementation and for prototypical reasons, we provided a small collection of very simple percussive and pitched `SynthDef`s in SuperCollider, including kick, snare, hi-hat, sine, bass, and harmonic synthesis. Their precise interpretation of $x$, $y$, and duration varies slightly, while generally mapping vertical position to pitch or spectral register, horizontal position to timbral or spatial properties, and duration to the temporal envelope. Also, using the same OSC representation for both layers keeps the sound engine independent from the process that generated the event: the distinction between entitys and grids therefore remains in their geographic and temporal behaviour rather than in the synthesis architecture.
Despite the current simplicity, this solution easily allows for quick integration with other SuperCollider-based ecosystems. 



## Browser-based interface

Finally, the browser interface combines live code and geographic representation within the same performance surface. The street map occupies the background, while the Python source is overlaid transparently with syntax highlighting and a text shadow for readability - Figure \ref{fig:full}. 
When declared, `entity` layers display their corresponding areas, street-following lines, or attraction points together with the moving entity. Whenever an entity reaches a relevant street node and generates an event, an expanding fading circle provides immediate visual feedback; on the other hand, `grid` layers display both the original places and their quantized cells. To avoid adding further motion over the map and saturate the visualization, the current sequencer step is represented at the UI left and bottom margins using illuminated cells. 
We also include several utility widgets for controlling the environment, including location selection, OSC transport, code reload and evaluation, and a presentation mode for hiding most interface elements. A compact panel at the bottom of the interface summarizes the currently active musical layers, reporting the main properties of each grid and entity together with their associated colour. Additionally, the performer can switch between code and map interaction modes, allowing the same surface to alternate between live editing and geographic navigation for easier map inspection.

# Usage examples and emerging properties
In this section, we would like to highlight some preliminary reflections that emerged from early exploration and testing with the presented prototype. Both authors have several years of experience with traditional pattern-based live coding and have regularly performed with frameworks such as Sonic Pi and TidalCycles. While this should not be intended as a proper evaluation based on scheduled sessions and a rigorous experimental framework, but more as an emergent reasoning developed through multiple individual sessions, we hope that these observations can still provide valuable insights into what we believe this system could contribute to evolving perspectives on live coding practices.

A first obvious consequence of the cartographic approach is that the same code can produce substantially different musical structures when evaluated over different maps. Entity trajectories depend on the local street topology, while grid patterns depend on the position and density of the selected POI. Consequently, structural decisions are highly delegated to the underlying map, and moving the same declarations to different cities has strong influence on both the temporal behaviour of the entitys and the spatial distribution of the sequenced events without modifying the code itself - Figure \ref{fig:maps}. A similar effect can be obtained by changing the map scale: zooming into a denser or sparser area effectively changes the geographic material from which the musical processes are derived; at the same time, it is also possible to use the speed / BPM parameter to force the system towards more controlled behaviour - and that's arguably the main point of intervention during an actual performance.

This also introduces a relationship between geographic and musical density: dense street networks, with equal speed or BPM, tend to generate more frequent entity events, while concentrated POI distributions yield more populated patterns. Therefore, map selection and zoom can indeed act as proper compositional transformations.

The distinction between temporized grids and asynchronous entitys is also not completely rigid. For instance, we noted that constraining an entity to a very small `area` or short `line` reduces its possible trajectories and can produce repetitive behaviours, approaching a conventional pattern; larger regions and less deterministic behaviours instead emphasize the irregularities of the underlying topology. Moreover, the addition of stochastic behaviours mean that the same map and code do not necessarily produce exactly the same performance on each execution.

Finally, by design, the choice of adding multiple layers allow regular and asynchronous temporalities to coexist. Grids can establish persistent metric structures, while entitys generate independent events around them - occasionally producing unplanned temporal alignments without sharing a common clock. Different cities can therefore expose markedly different musical characteristics from otherwise identical declarations, making geography itself part of the variability of the performance.


# Discussion

Previous work already connected maps and geographic data to forms of musical interaction [@krygier1994sound; @gaye2003sonic; @park2010composition], XYPI shifts the emphasis from *sonifying or navigating geographic information* toward using cartographic relations as constraints on musical organization. This distinction is visible against *GeoMuse Beta* and *Promenadologie* [@turbitt2024promenadologie], which indeed informed our initial design in complementary ways (see Section \ref{sec:motivation}). Specifically, the former suggested the possibility of treating geographic distributions as sequencer material, while the latter reinforced the idea of using geographically situated elements and their spatial relations as an active musical surface. In XYPI, however, these intuitions are brought together within two complementary live coded models.

This produces a useful tension with established pattern-based live-coding practices [@mclean2014tidal; @roos2023strudel]. With `grid`, the performer can still manipulates a recognizably pattern-oriented object: geography determines its content, but the resulting structure is metrically repeated and directly controlled through tempo and traversal parameters. `entity`, instead, shift the perspective towards behavioural and autonomous approaches [@blackwell2004selforganized; @bovermann2014computation; @roma2023entity; @roma2025flocks], since the performer does not - necessarily - explicitly construct musical sequences but specifies the conditions under which they can emerge. Consequently, we can argue that what is live coded is a changes in granularity: rather than defining which events occur when, the performer can define where movement is possible, how decisions are taken, and at what speed the resulting process unfolds. 

This partial displacement of event-level control also resonates with previous work on interactive live coding systems and changing performative roles. For instance, Dal Rì *et al.* observed a shift toward a more orchestrational role when the coder had to adapt to musical material continuously provided by another performer [@dalri2023sharing]; in XYPI, a comparable shift emerges instead from geographic constraints, which shape the process after code evaluation. Attanayake *et al.* transferred more explicit control to an entity capable of modifying and executing the performer's code [@attanayake2020disruption], whereas XYPI leaves code manipulation entirely to the performer and introduces uncertainty only in the subsequent unfolding of behaviours. This difference can be also framed through the dimensions of predictability and modifiability proposed by Xambó and Roma [@xambo2024agencies]: `grid` remains comparatively more predictable, while `entity` leaves the resulting event sequence contingent on local decisions. More broadly, this also suggests a particular distribution of agency within XYPI, as part of the event-level organization is delegated to the mutual interaction between declared behaviours and geographic structure. While an exaustive commentary on this point would require an in-depth evaluation, we support that agency is not entirely transferred to an autonomous musical partner, but distributed across different levels of the performance process [@xambo2022virtual; @xambo2024agencies].

The coexistence of these two layers exposes different relationships between geography and musical time. In `grid`, structure is projected into an external metric that is not aprioristically defined by the data; in `entity`, temporality emerges from geographic traversal. We can speculate that this also introduces an unusual form of contextual variability compared with traditional pattern-based environments. In frameworks such as TidalCycles or Strudel, for instance, patterns explicitly define relations between time and events [@mclean2014tidal; @roos2023strudel], so re-evaluating the same code generally preserves its organization (unless modifications of the patterns or the sound material are introduced). In XYPI, instead, re-evaluating identical declarations over another city or scale can generate completely different musical outcomes, making the geographic context itself part of the resulting musical structure.

A related distinction concerns indeed the role of visualization and scoring. Magnusson conceptualizes algorithms themselves as scores through which musical processes are composed and performed [@magnusson2011algorithms], while Masu and Dal Rì explored visual representations as complementary descriptive scores [@masu2023visual]. XYPI partially reverses this relation: the visual representation is not only an outcome or interpretative counterpart of an existing musical structure, but provides part of its structural foundation, making visualization simultaneously a performance interface and a generative form of scoring.

Finally, we argue that this geographic contingency is, in our view, compositionally interesting, but also complicates interpretation. Maps are selective and processual representations [@kitchin2007rethinking], and OpenStreetMap itself does not represent geographic space uniformly [@haklay2008openstreetmap; @haklay2010quality]. Differences between cities may therefore reflect not only their urban morphology, but also the way such morphology is encoded in the underlying dataset. The musical structures produced by XYPI should consequently be understood as emerging from both geographic space and its specific representation.



# Conclusions and Future Work
In this paper, we presented XYPI, a live coding environment in which geographic structures become part of the musical process through two complementary models: metrically organized infrastucture grids and asynchronous street-constrained entitys. By merging patterns, behaviours, and data, and by treating the visualized maps as a proper live codable operational structure, we hope that the system could contribute to broader perspectives on live coding, highlighting potentially novel perspective on the practice. 
Future work will primarily focus on a more systematic evaluation across performers and geographic contexts, investigating how users perceive and exploit the balance between predictable and emergent behaviours. Accordingly, we also plan to explore richer functions, mappings, and synthesis strategies.
